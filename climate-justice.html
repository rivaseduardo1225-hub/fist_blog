Climate justice

Climate change is not only a physical process. It is also a distribution of risk and responsibility — and the two are deeply unequal.

What climate justice asks

Sultana (2022) argues that climate justice cannot be separated from existing inequalities of class, gender and geography. The people least responsible for greenhouse-gas emissions are frequently the most exposed to their consequences — and the least equipped to recover.

For the Philippines, that is not an abstraction. The country absorbs some of the world's highest disaster risk while contributing less than 0.4% of global emissions. Klinsky & Mavrogianni (2023) add a second layer: debt distress and thin insurance markets leave less fiscal room to adapt, so the burden shifts onto households.

The justice questions in this report

  • Who is exposed to risk, and who can move away from it?
  • Who contributed most to historical emissions, and who pays now?
  • Who decides how adaptation money is spent, and on what?
A transition or adaptation that reproduces existing patterns of inequality is not fully just, however low its emissions may be.
